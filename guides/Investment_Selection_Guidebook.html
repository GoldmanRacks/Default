How I Think

I don’t start from a company, a sector or a banker’s book. I start from a structural shift in how the world allocates capital, follow it down through budgets, contracts and production lines until I find the constraint that has to be paid, and then pick the instrument (a private stake, a roll-up, a listed equity, an option or a rates trade) that lets me own that constraint at the right price.

1.1 One framework, not four disciplines

Macro, geopolitics, military doctrine, industrial supply chains and derivatives structure are a single analytical system to me, not separate specialties. A change in one domain propagates into the others: a Treasury funding decision changes the rate path, which changes the exit window for long-duration private assets; a cancelled diplomatic trip changes the timing on energy and volatility positions; an appropriations line changes the revenue of a tier-3 machine shop. I hold my theses as interlocking pillars. When one pillar is revised, I trace the cascade through every position that depends on it before I do anything else.

1.2 How the world actually works

  • Incentives, power and imperfect information drive outcomes. I analyze governments, primes, LPs and counterparties by what they are paid and pressured to do, not by what they say.
  • Institutional incompetence is a source of mispricing. Large allocators abandon sectors that are hard to underwrite, too small, politically awkward or operationally messy: family-owned industrials, ESOPs, blue-collar regional businesses, tier-3 defense suppliers. Those gaps create both real-economy damage and exploitable pricing. I target both at once: returns come from operational restoration, not financial engineering.
  • The theme is beta; the entry point is alpha. By the time a theme is obvious (rearmament, AI compute) it is priced in public markets. What I am paid for is capturing it earlier, deeper in the supply chain, or through a structure others can’t or won’t use.
  • Constraints change more slowly than narratives. I don’t need to be right about the whole macro story. I need to be right about which constraint binds first, because constraints are measurable and edges built on them decay slowly.

1.3 Second- and third-order thinking

The first-order trade is what everyone does after reading the headline. My process asks what happens after everyone does the first-order trade.

Headline First-order (consensus) Second / third-order (my process)
Defense budgets rise Buy primes, buy the hottest defense-tech round Throughput is set by tier-2/3 suppliers: motors, forgings, energetics, case machining. Own the constraint.
AI race with China Buy late-stage AI at peak marks If Taiwan is contested, the scarce asset is the domestic fail-safe stack: lithography, metrology, substrates, packaging.
Intelligence budget grows Ignore it, because it is classified In-Q-Tel is funded inside the intelligence budget. A bigger budget means a larger, government-validated deal pipeline to mirror.
Treasury fights long-end yields Buy duration, or short it Treasury is rewriting the rights attached to the bond. Own the release valve (gold) and the curve shape, and add duration only once repression is confirmed.
US–China decoupling Avoid China Pair it: long the allied side of the chokepoint, short the subsidized side.

1.4 Ten habits

01Constraint first. Geopolitics → budgets → contracts → capacity → equity value, with every link evidenced.
02Two layers down. The interesting opportunity sits several steps from the obvious trade.
03Physical over narrative. Requalifying a supplier takes years, so a physical moat outlasts a story moat.
04Compelled evidence. I underwrite what someone was legally required to disclose, not what they chose to say.
05Asymmetry. Design for convexity to the most mispriced scenario, then make sure the base case still pays.
06Discrete events stay discrete. Awards, IPOs, votes and recompetes are modeled as steps, not smoothed into growth.
07Structure solves access. Preferred, cornerstone, secondary blocks and roll-up rebrands open assets others skip.
08Pick the instrument, not just the idea. The expression should match the mechanism and the time horizon.
09Define the kill signal first. Before entry, I name the observable that would tell me I’m wrong.
10Honest tails. Scenario weights are fixed before I look at the output, and risk sections are analysis, not disclaimers.

What Makes a Target Selectable

2.1 The selection equation

Attractiveness = Demand engines × Constraint / moat × Evidence quality × Entry price × Access

The factors multiply. A near-zero on any one kills the target however strong the others are. A brilliant chokepoint with no route to a stake is a pass. A huge TAM at a mark that can’t clear the hurdle is a pass. A cheap price on a company with no demand engine is a value trap. When one factor is weak but curable, the target goes on a watchlist with the condition that would change it written down.

Factor Question I ask What “strong” looks like
Demand engines How many independent secular curves pull on this company, and how wide is the market? Two engines (Substrate: national security × AI), or breadth across every demographic and channel (Made In)
Constraint / moat What can the market not route around? Capacity, qualification, sole-source status, IP, brand credibility, network
Evidence quality Can the thesis be proven from compelled disclosures and priced marks? Contract awards, filings, priced rounds, secondary prints
Entry price Does the mark leave room for the Base case to clear the hurdle? Pre-IPO discount, compressed multiple, family / ESOP pricing, multiple arbitrage
Access Is there a real, structurable route to a meaningful position? Significant minority with board rights, cornerstone allocation, roll-up rebrand, liquid listed instrument

2.2 Eight selection lenses

Each lens is a different way a company can earn a place in the book. Most selected names pass through two or three.

How I Research

3.1 The signal stack

My research advantage comes from combining signal types that rarely sit on the same desk. I describe it as an OSINT-to-capital pipeline: open-source intelligence and compelled disclosure converted into positions faster than consensus models update.

Layer What I read Examples
Compelled disclosure What governments and companies must publish Appropriations and NDAA text (FY2027 bill read in full and synthesized), contract awards and IDIQ awardee lists, S-1s, the Fed H.4.1 release, daily Treasury cash balance
Open-source intelligence Physical-world signals before they hit filings ADS-B aviation pattern analysis, GPS-interference mapping, naval basing and submarine build-out, a Taiwan warning-indicator dashboard
Market microstructure Where money is positioning Unusual options flow (a systematic input, not a standalone signal), dealer gamma positioning, cross-expiry open-interest analysis
Operator and local intelligence What filings miss Site visits, management conversations, county board agendas and votes, local land-use and incentive decisions
Synthesis How signals interact Interlocking pillars: e.g. an Iran deal fracture → energy timing → volatility spreads → gold accumulation

3.3 The question stack

Research is a sequence of questions, each with its own evidence. A target moves to the next stage only when the current one is answered from primary sources. I never skip to price.

Stage Core questions Gate
1. Demand engines Who must buy this? How many independent curves drive it? How wide is the market across demographics, channels and geographies? A named dollar flow and an engine count
2. Constraint / moat What can’t the market route around? How long would replication take? Who else could supply? A chokepoint or a durable commercial moat, or a pass
3. Company proof Is revenue real and visible: contracts, cohorts, repeat rates, margins? A program-level or unit-level build
4. Price What is the mark, and which evidence tier supports it? Priced / secondary / contract-derived / estimate
5. Access & structure Can I get a meaningful position? Which structure protects me? Route, structure and size
6. Scenarios & exit What happens in Base, Bull, Bear and the tail? Who buys it, and when? Expected return, hurdle comparison, tripwires

3.4 Decomposing macro into constraint

The defining move in my process: I don’t express a macro view directly. I break it into the physical, technological, financial and institutional constraints that must clear before spending becomes output, and I buy the constraint.

Result: solid-rocket motors (L3Harris MSL), energetics (Helicon), missile-casing forgings (Ellwood, Superior & Grand Valley), warhead-case machining (Faxon), and in public markets the alloy, magnet, RF and coatings suppliers (CRS, MP, QRVO, PPG).

The full analytical chain I run on every thesis has twelve links: geopolitics → policy → appropriations → programs → production targets → capacity → bottleneck → supplier → ownership → entry structure → catalyst → payoff.

5.2 Program-level underwriting

Sleeve-level growth assumptions are placeholders. Every position eventually gets a bottom-up build: program revenue = driver growth + share gain, with discrete award steps, a margin ramp, FCF conversion and an exit multiple, producing an implied CAGR that feeds the sleeve. Proceeds are haircut for dilution (Check × (1 + CAGR)^T × retention).

  • Faxon Machining ($9M for 9.8% at a $92M post): eight program lines (BLU-136/B, MOP, NGP, Tomahawk, penetrator IDIQ, missile structures, FMS, commercial), each tied to a named driver. The entry was cross-checked against a contract-derived valuation: Bear $21M / Base $76M / Bull $194M at 25/50/25 = $91.75M. Fair, not a discount.
  • L3Harris Missile Solutions ($15M pre-IPO): 2027E EBIT $574M × 22x = $12.6B IPO value, less a 15% pre-IPO discount = $10.7B entry. Program build across PAC-3, THAAD, Standard Missile, Tomahawk, GMLRS / PrSM, hypersonics and Golden Dome interceptors.

5.3 Case study: Substrate, two engines in one asset

Lens: dual-engine convergence

Engine 1, national security: domestic X-ray lithography and US fabs, a path to leading-edge chips if Taiwan / TSMC access is lost; In-Q-Tel backed; CFIUS-clean and acquirable by government or primes. Engine 2, the AI boom: AI accelerators consume leading-edge wafers, fab capacity is the binding constraint, and the technology aims to lower cost per leading-edge wafer.

Why selected: if the AI cycle cools, the national-security need remains; if geopolitics calms, the AI demand remains. That gives a high floor on strategic value with convex upside, and the highest composite score in the book (4.85).

What must be true: production-grade yield and throughput; fab financing and anchor offtake; a timeline inside the hold. Risks: binary technology (Bear 0.32x at 5 years); capital intensity and dilution; a >$1B entry mark.

Decision: anchor position ($80M, 7.6%), capped at ~32% of the sleeve. The dual engine justifies the largest check; the binary risk justifies the cap.

5.4 Case study: Made In Cookware, TAM breadth across demographics

Lens: TAM breadth

Demographic breadth: household formation (first kitchens), the affluent upgrade and replacement cycle, gifting and wedding registries, home cooks trading up. Channel breadth: DTC, wholesale and retail placements, and restaurant and pro-chef placements, where professional use builds consumer trust. Room to extend into knives, bakeware and tools, and to grow internationally.

Why selected: a universal category reachable through every cohort and channel without category education. Capital efficiency (~$8.3M raised in total) signals self-funded unit economics and low dilution.

Honest read: the mark (~$280M) is the least-grounded in its sleeve, since no secondary market exists; import and tariff exposure hurts in the Shock case; and the Base IRR (~11–12%) sits below my ~15% position hurdle. It is held for TAM optionality (Bull 4.2x at 7 years), and the mark has to be confirmed through company financials before any upsizing.

5.5 Case study: EUV Tech, the chokepoint with a floor

Lens: chokepoint + downside protection

At-wavelength EUV metrology is a node no leading-edge fab can route around. The company already generates revenue as a niche leader, so the Bear case is roughly capital-preserving (~1.0x), the best risk-adjusted name in the deep-tech sleeve. A ~17% stake earns a board seat. Its niche market caps the Base case at 14% CAGR, but it is selected for asymmetry: it offsets the binary risk in the anchor.

5.6 Case study: reading the secondary tape

The Farmer’s Dog: compounding with a catalyst

Profitable, >$1B revenue, a daily-use subscription. Forge prints imply $3.76B, up from a $2.5B Series E. The Walmart.com launch moves it from DTC-only to omnichannel. Bear CAGR is still +10%: the only consumer name above 1.0x in the Bear case.

Thrive Market: growth without return

Revenue rose from $400M to $700M+, yet Forge implies ~$945M, down ~27% from the 2021 Series C. The 2021 entry multiple was the problem. Lesson: price the tape, not the last round. Growth doesn’t equal return when the entry was set at peak multiples.

Arena II: Operational Roll-ups

The same constraint-and-entry logic, applied to small, founder-run businesses that institutional capital ignores.(3)

6.1 Case study: the Mid-Atlantic marina platform

Lenses: multiple arbitrage · defense-corridor demand · operational restoration

Thesis: buy single-site marinas from founder-operators at low multiples, institutionalize them as a platform, and exit at a platform multiple. Initial sites in Southern Maryland (Desoto’s Landing on the Patuxent at the Route 231 bridge, Lens Marina, Boatel California, Vera’s Beach Club, Stoney’s Broomes Island), expanding to Virginia Beach / Norfolk and coastal North Carolina.

Demand engine: the defense corridor, with NAVAIR Patuxent River in Southern Maryland and the Hampton Roads naval cluster: durable, government-anchored household income near the water.

Restoration, not replacement: sites keep their names and local roots; the roll-up is a rebrand-light platform with shared back office, procurement and financing. That preserves local goodwill (the asset a strategic buyer pays for) while removing the cost disadvantages of independence.

Catalysts and re-underwriting dates. 6.2 The second vertical: premium fitness at mass pricing

An Equinox-style experience at Planet-Fitness-level pricing (~$40–80 a month), with sauna, cold plunge and spa therapies as high-margin add-ons, in the same target geographies. Same structure: partner with local gyms, owners keep their platform, the deal is a rebrand. The TAM-breadth lens applies: premium wellness has historically been priced for a narrow demographic, and this concept widens it.

Arena IV: The Macro Overlay

Selection applies to instruments as much as to companies: choose the expression whose payoff matches the mechanism, stage entries on confirmation, and name the observable that kills it.(3)

8.1 Thesis: financial repression by rewriting the bond

Formed in November 2025: the Treasury is suppressing long-end yields not by cutting coupons but by rewriting the rights attached to the bond (liquidity, collateral, expected supply, access, custody). The mechanisms: enlarged long-end buybacks; the Fed’s foreign repo facility widened to more central banks; Fed bill purchases (~$40B a month since December 2025); the Treasury cash balance as a buyback funding source; and stablecoin legislation that makes issuers structural bill buyers from January 2027.

The release valve is gold certificate revaluation under 31 U.S.C. §5117: 261.5M troy ounces carried at the $42.2222 statutory price, worth over $1T at market, could be marked up to fund long-end buybacks without new issuance and without Fed cooperation. The same mechanism surfaced independently in a widely circulated essay in September 2026, about nine months after the thesis was formed.

8.2 Choosing the expression

Expression Why this instrument Rule
GLD call ladder, all strikes in-the-money Convex to revaluation and to negative real rates; ITM limits theta Monetize ahead of the midterm window
2s30s flattener Expresses curve compression from long-end repression, not a generic gold or duration bet Hold while buyback scale grows
Long duration The payoff only exists once repression is real Enter after repression is confirmed, not anticipated

Main risk, stated in advance: the market reads a visible revaluation as debt monetization and bear-steepens the long end. That breaks the thesis rather than confirming it. Secondary risks: the Treasury Secretary’s on-record denial, legal ambiguity over whether the statutory price needs Congress, and Fed–Treasury friction.

9.2 Sizing and construction

  • Cap the anchor. Maximum conviction still gets a cap when the risk is binary (Substrate ≈32% of its sleeve).
  • Tranche ladder. Anchor → growth core → pre-IPO / liquid → venture → seed optionality, with size falling as binary risk rises.
  • More than 15% ownership means seeking a board seat or observer rights.
  • Trim versus new money. A substitute exposure is funded by trimming (Faxon came from Superior & Grand Valley); additive exposure gets a new commitment (MSL took the Weapons sleeve from $390M to $405M).
  • Allied and foreign names stay minority-only and smaller, because the CFIUS ceiling limits exit buyers.
  • Instrument sizing in the liquid books: ITM LEAPS and defined-risk structures, so the maximum loss is known at entry and theta does not decide the outcome.
