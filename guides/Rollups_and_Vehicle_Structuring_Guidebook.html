01

Why Structure Is Strategy

Most investment failures that aren’t thesis failures are structure failures: a liquid book trapped in an illiquid wrapper, an illiquid asset in a vehicle that promises monthly redemptions, a tax-exempt LP hit with unrelated business income, a fund that can’t legally be marketed to the investors who want it. This guide covers two levers: roll-ups, which create value inside the asset, and vehicles, which decide whether that value reaches investors intact.

1.1 Four principles

01Match liquidity both ways. Investor redemption terms must never be more generous than the assets can support. Daily or monthly dealing is for listed instruments; private stakes need committed, locked capital.
02Wrap for the investor, not the manager. Domicile, legal form and regime are chosen by who must be able to buy (professional, semi-professional or retail; EU or US; taxable or tax-exempt).
03Separate what fails differently. Different risk, liquidity and tax profiles go in different vehicles or compartments, so one can’t contaminate another.
04Buy the platform, not just the asset. In roll-ups, value comes from what the platform does to each acquisition: cost of capital, procurement, systems and the exit multiple a scaled business commands.

02

Roll-ups: How Buy-and-Build Creates Value

Part A · Roll-ups

A roll-up buys a platform, then acquires and integrates smaller add-ons. It works because small businesses sell at lower multiples than scaled platforms, and a platform runs each business better than its founder could alone.

2.1 The five value levers

Lever Mechanism Durability
Multiple arbitrage Buy add-ons at small-business multiples; the combined EBITDA is valued at a platform multiple on exit Real but cyclical: it depends on exit markets and shrinks as competition for add-ons rises
Cost synergies Shared procurement, insurance, finance, HR, IT, marketing; better terms from scale High if executed; easiest to verify
Revenue synergies Cross-selling, pricing discipline, cross-site memberships, filling capacity Valuable but slow; underwrite conservatively
Cost of capital Platform borrows cheaper and longer than a single site; can unlock real estate value (OpCo / PropCo) High once scale is reached
Operational restoration Professional management, systems and capex in businesses starved of both The deepest moat, and the core of my approach

2.2 The arithmetic of arbitrage

Arbitrage value = Acquired EBITDA × (Exit multiple − Entry multiple)

On the marina platform model, a 5.8x entry and 9.0x base-case exit mean each $1 of acquired EBITDA adds $3.20 of enterprise value at exit before any improvement, a 1.55x uplift on price. The exit multiple is therefore the most important roll-up assumption, so I always show returns with the arbitrage stripped out (§5).

2.3 What makes a good roll-up market

Test What I look for Marina / fitness application
Fragmentation Many small owner-operators, no dominant consolidator Single-site marinas and independent gyms
Succession pressure Founders near retirement without heirs or a buyer Long-tenured family operators
Durable demand node Demand anchored by something that doesn’t move Defense corridor: NAVAIR Patuxent River, the Hampton Roads naval cluster
Local moat Licenses, scarce sites or permits that can’t easily be replicated Waterfront slips and zoning are scarce by nature
Repeatable unit economics Each site has a similar P&L that systems can improve Slip rental, storage, fuel, service; memberships plus add-ons
Platform exit buyers Larger sponsors or strategics who pay up for scale Institutional marina and leisure platforms; fitness franchisors
03

Integration Models

The decision that most often separates successful roll-ups from failed ones is how much to integrate.

Model What changes Strengths Risks
Full integration One brand, one P&L, one system; sites become branches Maximum cost synergy; clean story for buyers Destroys local goodwill; founder and staff attrition; slow and costly
Federated / rebrand-light Sites keep names and local identity; platform centralizes the back office Preserves goodwill and staff; faster close; founders stay engaged Lower headline synergy; needs strong central reporting
Holding-company Minimal integration; capital allocation only Fast and cheap Little operational value created; weakest exit multiple

My default is federated. In the marina platform, sites such as Desoto’s Landing, Vera’s Beach Club and Stoney’s Broomes Island keep their names and roots; the roll-up is a rebrand-light platform. In the fitness vertical, local gym owners keep their platform and the deal is a rebrand. Local goodwill is the asset a strategic buyer ultimately pays for, so preserving it is value-maximizing, not sentimental.

3.1 What to centralize, what to leave local

Centralize Leave local
Finance, treasury, financing and insurance Customer relationships and site management
Procurement (fuel, parts, equipment, consumables) Brand name, community presence, events
Technology: booking, POS, CRM, reporting Day-to-day staffing and service culture
Pricing analytics and revenue management Local pricing within platform guardrails
Capex planning and real estate strategy Operating hours, seasonal programming

How Roll-ups Are Structured and Financed

4.1 Deal structures

Tool What it does Why it’s used
Rollover equity Seller reinvests part of proceeds into the platform Aligns founders who stay; bridges price gaps
Earn-out Part of the price depends on future performance When seller projections exceed my underwriting
Seller note Seller finances part of the price, subordinated to senior debt Lowers cash at close; signals seller confidence
Management equity / MIP Equity for site and platform managers Retaining key operators through integration
Asset vs stock purchase Asset deals give a tax basis step-up and leave liabilities behind Default for small targets where feasible

US tax note: for S-corporation targets, an F reorganization or a §338(h)(10) election can give the buyer an asset-style tax basis step-up while keeping contracts and licenses in place.

4.2 How platforms are financed

  • Acquisition and delayed-draw term loans (DDTL) pre-commit debt for future add-ons, so each deal doesn’t need fresh financing.
  • Leverage on pro forma EBITDA: lenders size debt on platform EBITDA including acquired sites, which is why add-backs matter so much: inflated "adjusted EBITDA" is the classic roll-up failure point.
  • OpCo / PropCo split: where the business owns valuable real estate (waterfront land, buildings), separating property from operations can release capital through a sale-leaseback or property-level financing, at the cost of rent and reduced control.
  • Equity cadence: sponsors typically commit equity for the platform plus a first wave of add-ons, because funding add-ons purely with debt becomes fragile when rates rise.

5.1 Strip out the arbitrage

The test I apply to every roll-up: does it still work if the exit multiple doesn’t expand?

Exit multiple Exit EV Exit equity MOIC IRR
9.0x (base) $126.0M $107.0M 3.69x 29.8%
7.0x (half the expansion) $97.9M $78.9M 2.72x 22.2%
5.8x (no expansion) $81.1M $62.1M 2.14x 16.4%

At no expansion the platform still clears a ~15% hurdle on synergies, growth and paydown alone. That is the standard: arbitrage is upside, not the thesis. A roll-up that only works at a higher exit multiple is a bet on markets, not a business plan.

How Roll-ups Fail

6.1 Common failure modes

Failure mode Early warning How strong platforms avoid it
Add-back inflation Adjusted EBITDA far above reported; "run-rate" synergies counted before they’re achieved QoE on every material add-on; lend and price on achieved synergies only
Overpaying as competition rises Add-on multiples creeping toward the platform multiple Walk-away multiple per deal; proprietary sourcing
Integration overload Reporting delays, staff turnover, customer complaints Pace acquisitions to integration capacity; central team built first
Founder and key-person loss Founders disengage after cash-out Rollover equity, earn-outs, management equity
Leverage into a downturn Covenant headroom thinning Conservative leverage; DDTL headroom; equity reserve
Exit multiple compression Platform buyers retreat; rates rise Underwrite to no expansion (§5.1)
Local political and land-use risk Zoning, permits, major local projects Treat key local decisions as scheduled re-underwrite dates

Local events matter as much as markets. For the marina platform, the Calvert County data-center process (land swap and park proposal, an AWS application withdrawn in August 2026, the state impact analysis and the November 2026 election) is a re-underwriting point: it can move land values, traffic and household income around the sites. A dark-fiber corridor to NAVAIR Patuxent River makes dual-use compute near Calvert Cliffs an upside scenario worth tracking.

6.2 The fitness vertical

An Equinox-style experience at Planet-Fitness-level pricing (~$40–80 a month), with sauna, cold plunge and spa therapies as high-margin add-ons. The metrics that matter differ from marinas: member churn, cost to acquire a member, lifetime value, ancillary revenue per member, and utilization by hour. The same federated model applies: partner with local gyms, let owners keep their platform, rebrand, and centralize the back office.

The Luxembourg Toolkit

Part B · Vehicles

Luxembourg is the default EU domicile for cross-border funds because it offers every regime on one menu, a deep service-provider ecosystem, and EU marketing passports.(3)

7.1 Regimes

Regime Who can invest CSSF authorization Key constraints Typical use
UCITS (Part I, 2010 Law) Retail, EU passport Yes, product-level 5 / 10 / 40 diversification; global exposure capped (commitment ≤100% NAV, or VaR limits); dealing at least twice a month; eligible assets only, so no direct commodities Liquid retail funds
Part II UCI (2010 Law) Retail in Luxembourg; an AIF under AIFMD Yes, product-level Flexible risk-spreading set by CSSF circulars; alternative strategies permitted Liquid and semi-liquid alternatives, options strategies
SIF (2007 Law) Well-informed investors Yes Risk-spreading principle (generally ≤30% in one issuer) Professional funds needing a regulated label
SICAR (2004 Law) Well-informed investors Yes Must invest in risk capital; no diversification rule Private equity and venture
RAIF (2016 Law) Well-informed investors No: must appoint an authorized external AIFM Flexible; can opt into the SICAR-style regime Fast-to-market alternative funds
Unregulated SCSp / SCS Professional investors (via AIFM if AIFMD applies) No Contractual freedom; partnership agreement governs Private equity, infrastructure, co-invest

Subscription tax (taxe d’abonnement): 0.05% a year of NAV for UCITS and Part II funds; 0.01% for SIFs and RAIFs (with exemptions for some classes); SICARs and SICAR-style RAIFs are exempt. Minimum capital for regulated funds is €1.25M, reached within the period the law sets for each regime.

7.2 Legal forms

Form What it is Notes
SICAV Company with variable capital: shares issued and redeemed at NAV The standard open-ended corporate form; has legal personality
SICAF Company with fixed capital Closed-ended corporate form
FCP Contractual co-ownership managed by a ManCo No legal personality; tax-transparent in many jurisdictions
SCSp Special limited partnership No legal personality; tax-transparent; the PE workhorse

A regime and a form combine: a fund can be a Part II SICAV, a SIF-SCSp, a RAIF-SICAV and so on. Umbrella structures hold several compartments (sub-funds) with legally segregated assets and liabilities, each with its own strategy, investors and NAV.

How a SICAV Works

8.1 Who does what

Role Responsibility Leviathan liquid SICAV (planned)(2)
Management company / AIFM Regulatory substance, risk management, compliance, valuation oversight, distribution oversight Third-party ManCo under a white-label arrangement
Investment manager / sponsor Portfolio decisions, under delegation from the ManCo Leviathan retains investment discretion as sponsor
Depositary Safekeeping, cash monitoring, oversight of NAV and dealing Luxembourg credit institution
Central administration / TA NAV calculation, shareholder register, subscriptions and redemptions Appointed through the platform
Prime broker Financing, margin and execution for the derivatives overlay Separate, independently negotiated mandate
Auditor Annual audit Luxembourg-approved auditor
Board of directors Governance and oversight of all delegates Including independent directors

8.2 The white-label model

A white-label (third-party ManCo) platform lets a new sponsor launch a regulated Luxembourg fund without building its own authorized ManCo: substance, compliance, risk and regulatory reporting are rented, and the sponsor keeps the portfolio decisions. Trade-offs: platform fees and less control over operational choices; the ManCo has oversight rights and can veto activity outside the risk framework. When to graduate: once AUM and product count justify building a Chapter 15 ManCo or an authorized AIFM of one’s own.

Options Overlays Inside a SICAV

Vehicle 1 is planned as a liquid book with a monthly rolling options overlay and a futures sleeve (§10). The wrapper decides which overlays are allowed.

9.1 Overlay strategies

Strategy Mechanics Payoff effect Use
Covered calls Sell out-of-the-money calls against long positions, rolled monthly Income; caps upside on the covered shares Range-bound or high implied-volatility regimes
Protective puts / put spreads Buy puts (or put spreads to cut cost) Defined downside; costs premium Event risk; tail protection
Collars Long put financed by a short call Bounded range; low or zero net premium Protecting gains in concentrated positions
Long calls / LEAPS Buy in-the-money or long-dated calls instead of stock Leveraged upside, limited theta when ITM Expressing high-conviction theses capital-efficiently

9.2 UCITS versus Part II for an overlay

Issue UCITS Part II
Global exposure Commitment approach ≤100% of NAV, or VaR (absolute ≤20%; relative ≤2× benchmark) Set by prospectus within CSSF risk-spreading guidance
Cover rules Every derivative must be covered by the underlying or liquid assets More flexibility, including uncovered or leveraged positions within stated limits
Counterparty limits (OTC) 10% of NAV for credit institutions, 5% otherwise Set by risk framework
Short exposure Synthetic only (no physical short sales) Wider latitude
Dealing At least twice a month As the prospectus sets: monthly suits a monthly roll
Commodity exposure Only via eligible diversified commodity indices or delta-one ETCs; no single-commodity futures Direct listed commodity futures and options on futures, within prospectus limits
Distribution Retail passport across the EU Retail in Luxembourg; professional marketing in the EU under the AIFMD passport

Why Part II for this book: a monthly roll, LEAPS-style leverage and commodity futures fit Part II better than UCITS rules, and monthly dealing matches the overlay cycle.

9.3 What can go wrong

  • Assignment and pin risk: short calls can be exercised near expiry, so rolls usually happen before expiry week.
  • Gap risk: covered calls add income but don’t protect the downside.
  • Margin: haircuts rise in volatile markets and can force sales at the worst time.
  • Behavioral drift: income-seeking overwriting can quietly turn a fund into a short-volatility strategy.

Commodities and Futures Inside the Liquid SICAV

Yes: a Part II SICAV can hold listed commodity and financial futures, and options on futures, alongside its equity and options book. That lets one regulated vehicle carry both the equity overlay and the macro expressions (gold, rates curve, energy, FX) that would otherwise need separate accounts or wrappers.(3)

10.1 What each regime allows

Exposure UCITS Part II
Financial futures (equity index, rates, FX) Permitted, within global-exposure and cover rules Permitted, within prospectus limits
Single-commodity futures (gold, crude, metals) Not permitted Permitted, listed and exchange-cleared
Commodity index exposure Only via indices meeting financial-index diversification rules (generally ≤20% per component, one up to 35%), through swaps or index futures Permitted directly or via indices
Delta-one ETCs Eligible as transferable securities if they embed no derivative Permitted
Options on futures Financial underlyings only Financial and commodity underlyings
Physical delivery Not permitted Avoided in practice: positions are rolled or closed before delivery

CSSF Circular 02/80 contains specific risk-spreading rules for Part II funds that invest in futures and options; the prospectus sets the fund’s own limits within them.

10.2 What a futures sleeve adds

Thesis Futures expression Compared with the equity / ETF route
Gold revaluation, negative real rates COMEX gold futures (100 oz; micro 10 oz) and options on gold futures No ETF fee and lower capital usage than GLD calls; adds roll and margin management
Long-end financial repression (2s30s flattener) 2-year Treasury futures against ultra-bond futures, weighted so DV01s match A pure curve trade, cleaner and cheaper than combining bond ETFs or TBT
Iran / Hormuz energy tail WTI or Brent crude futures and options Direct exposure to the commodity rather than to energy equities’ operating leverage
Yen carry unwind CME yen futures (or options on them) Cleaner than FXY; leverage and expiry are chosen to match the thesis horizon
Equity beta management Equity index futures and index options Fast hedging of the long book without selling core positions

10.3 How futures change the mechanics

  • Margin and collateral. Futures need only initial and variation margin, so most of the sleeve’s capital sits as collateral, typically in Treasury bills that earn the short rate. In a financial-repression regime that collateral yield is itself part of the return.
  • Roll yield. Holding futures means rolling them. In contango (later contracts more expensive) the roll costs money; in backwardation it earns money. Gold is usually in mild contango; crude flips between the two.
  • Delivery. Physically settled contracts (gold, crude, Treasuries) are rolled or closed before first notice day, so the fund never takes delivery.
  • Leverage measurement. Futures add notional exposure far above the margin posted. The prospectus defines a leverage measure (gross and commitment under AIFMD), and the ManCo’s risk function monitors it daily.
  • Clearing. Futures clear through a futures commission merchant (FCM) or clearing broker, often alongside but separate from the prime broker that finances the equity book.
  • US regulatory touchpoint. Trading US-listed futures can raise CFTC commodity-pool-operator questions for the sponsor; these are usually addressed through an available exemption.
  • NAV. Futures are marked to settlement prices daily, which fits monthly dealing comfortably.

Beyond Luxembourg: Other Wrappers and Cross-Border Issues

11.1 Domicile comparison

Domicile / wrapper Strengths Typical investors
Luxembourg (SICAV, RAIF, SCSp) Full regime menu; EU passports; deep provider base EU and global institutions, private banks, wealth platforms
Ireland (ICAV, QIAIF) Fast QIAIF authorization; US check-the-box friendly ICAV Institutional and hedge-fund-style products
Cayman Islands Familiar to US and Asian investors; flexible; tax-neutral Hedge funds, master-feeder structures
Delaware LP Standard for US investors; tax-transparent US taxable investors and the US side of master-feeders

11.2 Cross-border tax and marketing issues

Issue What happens Common solution
US taxable investors in a non-US corporate fund A SICAV is generally a PFIC for US tax purposes: punitive tax unless a QEF or mark-to-market election is available Delaware feeder or parallel vehicle; provide QEF statements
US tax-exempt investors Leverage and operating businesses create UBTI Offshore corporate blocker or feeder
Non-US investors in US operating assets ECI exposure Blocker corporations
ERISA plans 25% plan-asset test Monitor benefit-plan percentage; VCOC structures
EU marketing AIFMD passport for authorized AIFMs to EU professional investors; national private placement for others Choose an AIFM with passport rights; register before marketing
Retail access to illiquid assets Retail rules restrict illiquid funds ELTIF 2.0 (applies from January 2024): long-term assets with retail access and liquidity tools

AIFMD II (transposition deadline April 2026) adds loan-origination rules, tighter delegation oversight and mandatory liquidity-management tools for open-ended AIFs, which is relevant for any semi-liquid vehicle.

Long-Dated Vehicles

12.1 Holdco versus fund versus evergreen

Vehicle type Capital Life Fits
Closed-end fund (SCSp / SICAR) Committed, called over time ~10 years + extensions Private equity with a harvest cycle
Holding company Permanent capital on the balance sheet Indefinite Long holds, sleeves, flexible recycling (the Leviathan core PE model)
Evergreen / semi-liquid Periodic subscriptions and capped redemptions Indefinite Wealth channels; requires liquidity tools and a cash sleeve
Infrastructure vehicle Committed, long-dated ~10–15+ years Contracted, inflation-linked cash flows
Continuation vehicle Rolled assets from an earlier vehicle New term Extending the hold on winners
Co-invest SPV Deal-specific Life of the deal Larger checks; low-fee LP exposure

12.2 The infrastructure vehicle

A ~10-year vehicle suits assets whose value accrues slowly and predictably: contracted revenue, real estate and digital infrastructure. Structure it as a closed-ended partnership (typically an SCSp, regulated as a RAIF or SIF if investors require it) with a long investment period, distribution-focused economics and inflation linkage. Defense-corridor digital infrastructure (dark fiber and dual-use compute near government nodes) connects it to the roll-up thesis.
